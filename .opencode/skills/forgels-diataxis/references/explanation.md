# Explanation

Source: https://diataxis.fr/explanation/ (fetched 2026-10-18)

Explanation is a discursive treatment of a subject that permits reflection. It is understanding-oriented: it deepens and broadens understanding, brings clarity, light, and context. Reflection happens AFTER something else and shines new light on it.

Its perspective is higher and wider than the other three types. Its scope is a topic: an area of knowledge bounded in a reasonable, meaningful way. For the user it joins things together and answers "Can you tell me about ...?". It is the only kind of documentation it can make sense to read away from the product.

Alternative names: Discussion, Background, Conceptual guides, Topics. It is less urgent than the other three but no less important; without it, the practitioner's knowledge stays loose, fragmented, and anxious.

## Writing good explanation

- Make connections to other things, even outside the immediate topic, to weave understanding.
- Provide background and context: design decisions, historical reasons, technical constraints, implications, examples.
- Talk ABOUT the subject: the bigger picture, history, choices, alternatives, why. Titles should accept an implicit "about": "About user authentication".
- Admit opinion and perspective. Explanation can weigh alternatives and contrary opinions; understanding comes from a standpoint.
- Keep explanation closely bounded. Do not let instruction or technical description creep in; they belong in the other quadrants. Use a real or imagined "why" question as the prompt to bound the topic.

## Language

- "The reason for x is because historically, y ...": explain.
- "W is better than z, because ...": offer judgements and opinions where appropriate.
- "An x in system y is analogous to a w in system z. However ...": give context.
- "Some users prefer w (because z). This can be a good approach, but ...": weigh alternatives.
- "An x interacts with a y as follows: ...": unfold the machinery's internal secrets.
