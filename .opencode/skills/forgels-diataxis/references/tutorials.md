# Tutorials

Source: https://diataxis.fr/tutorials/ (fetched 2026-10-18)

A tutorial is an experience under the guidance of a tutor. It is always learning-oriented: a practical activity where the student learns by doing something meaningful toward an achievable goal. A tutorial is a lesson.

What the student DOES is not necessarily what they LEARN. Through doing they acquire facts, understanding, familiarity, names of things, tools, workflows, concepts.

## The teacher contract

Nearly all responsibility falls on the teacher: what the pupil learns, what they do to learn it, and their success. The pupil's only duty is attention and following directions. The exercise must be meaningful, successful, logical, and usefully complete.

## Key principles

- Do not try to teach. Provide an experience through which learning can happen. Give the learner things to DO.
- Show where they will be going at the start: describe what they will accomplish, never "In this tutorial you will learn ...".
- Deliver visible results early and often. Every step produces a comprehensible result, however small.
- Maintain a narrative of the expected: "You will notice that ...". Show actual or exact expected output. Flag likely failure signs.
- Point out what the learner should notice (prompts, changes) to close learning loops.
- Encourage repetition: make steps repeatable where possible.
- Ruthlessly minimise explanation. A tutorial is not the place for explanation; link to it instead. "We're using HTTPS because it's more secure" is enough.
- Focus on the concrete: this problem, this action, this result. General patterns emerge from concrete examples.
- Ignore options and alternatives. One path to a successful conclusion.
- Aspire to perfect reliability: the tutorial must work for every user, every time. Test with real users; you cannot rescue them mid-lesson.

## Language

- "We ...": first-person plural, tutor and learner together.
- "In this tutorial, we will ...": describe what the learner will accomplish.
- "First, do x. Now, do y.": no ambiguity.
- Minimal action rationale with a link: "We must do x before y because ... (see Explanation)".
- "The output should look something like ...": set expectations.
- "Notice that ... Remember that ... Let's check ...": confirm they are on track.
- Close by describing what they accomplished.
